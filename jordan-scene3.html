Jordan’s story · Scene 3 of 3

Which information can I trust?

A seizure after birth needs emergency help. Call 911.

Jordan is home and following her care plan. Her healthcare team explained that she had postpartum preeclampsia, but she wants to understand more.

Searching online, she finds another word: eclampsia. She also finds hospital information, personal recovery stories, and articles about supplements.

Some sources include research and professional advice, but their recommendations do not always agree.

“How do I decide what is trustworthy—and what applies to me?”

Make sense of the words

Match each term with its meaning by choosing an explanation.

Look beyond the headline

Jordan opens three search results. Each contains information that sounds convincing.

“What supports the advice, and is it about someone in my situation?”

These cards are fictional examples created for this activity.

Card A · Hospital information page

Understanding preeclampsia after birth

A hospital maternity service explains postpartum preeclampsia, warning signs, medicines, and follow-up.

The page identifies its clinical reviewers, includes a recent review date, and links to professional guidance.

It recommends following an individual care plan and discussing treatment changes with the prescribing team.

Card B · Personal health account

My recovery after postpartum preeclampsia: what helped me

A parent describes tracking her blood pressure, attending follow-up appointments, and making lifestyle changes. She links to a hospital resource and explains that her clinician eventually stopped her medication.

She adds:

“If your readings are improving, you may be ready to stop medication too. You could try a few days without it and keep monitoring.”

Card C · Commercial wellness article

Supporting healthy blood pressure after pregnancy

A supplement company publishes an article credited to a registered dietitian. It discusses nutrition and cites studies about ingredients associated with lower blood pressure in adults.

The article recommends its supplement as:

“A research-informed option that may reduce reliance on prescription medication during postpartum recovery.”

It does not show whether the product has been studied in people with postpartum preeclampsia.

Which source is the strongest starting point for understanding Jordan’s postpartum care?

What makes information useful?

Open each bubble to explore a question Jordan can ask.

Who is responsible?

Is the author or organization identified? Do they have expertise relevant to this topic?

A qualified professional can still make a claim that needs checking.

What does the evidence actually show?

Does the research support the recommendation being made? Was it about the same condition, population, and treatment?

One person’s experience or a study of an ingredient is not the same as evidence that a treatment works for Jordan.

Is it current and relevant?

When was the information reviewed? Does it discuss care after birth?

Reliable general information may still differ from Jordan’s individual care plan.

What is its purpose?

Is the source explaining options, sharing an experience, or selling something?

Notice promises, missing limitations, and whether benefits receive more attention than risks.

Before changing the plan

Jordan has been feeling better. She wonders whether the advice about stopping medication could apply to her.

“Could I just try it and see?”
What should Jordan do with this advice?

Jordan could ask:

“I read this online, but it sounds different from my instructions. Can we look at the evidence together and discuss whether it applies to me?”

Good questions are part of informed care.

Jordan saves the hospital resource and writes down the claims she wants to discuss. She continues her agreed care plan and brings her questions to follow-up.

She does not need to understand every medical word. She can ask who supports a claim, what the evidence shows, and how it relates to her recovery.
