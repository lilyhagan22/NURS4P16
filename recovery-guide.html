Getting to know your recovery

Recovery and warning signs

Learn about physical changes after birth, ways to care for yourself, and signs that mean you should ask for medical help.

This page summarizes the Public Health Agency of Canada’s postpartum recovery table. Recovery varies, so follow your own discharge instructions and care plan. These examples do not cover every possible complication.

If symptoms are severe or you feel unsafe, seek emergency help. Do not wait to finish reading this page. See when help cannot wait.

Fever: when to contact your healthcare provider

The Canada.ca guide lists a temperature of 38.0°C or higher measured in the ear, or 37.6°C or higher measured by mouth or under the arm, as a reason to contact your healthcare provider. Follow any instructions your healthcare team has given you about checking your temperature.

Bleeding and vaginal discharge

Changes you may notice

  • Bleeding and discharge can continue for up to 6 weeks after either a vaginal or caesarean birth.
  • First few days: discharge is usually red and heavier than later in recovery. Small clots may occur.
  • After a few days: bleeding should decrease and become pink or brown.
  • After about 10 days: discharge gradually becomes lighter, then white or yellow.

What can help?

  • Use pads rather than tampons or a menstrual cup.
  • Change your pad whenever you use the bathroom, and at least every 4 hours.
  • Gently rinse your perineum, the area between your vagina and anus, with warm water from a squeeze bottle.
  • Do not rinse inside your vagina or douche.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • Bleeding that becomes heavier instead of gradually decreasing, or a pad soaked in an hour.
  • Blood clots larger than a plum or an egg.
  • Discharge with a bad smell, ongoing abdominal pain, or fever.

If you soak a pad in less than an hour or pass large clots, seek emergency care immediately. If bleeding is very heavy and you feel faint or dizzy, call 911. Do not wait for a second soaked pad or drive yourself.

Perineal soreness, tears, and stitches

Changes you may notice

  • The perineum is the area between your vagina and anus. Stretching and tearing are common; you may also have had an episiotomy, a cut made during birth.
  • Bruising, swelling, and pain should gradually improve.
  • Dissolving stitches may take about 2 to 4 weeks to disappear. You may notice small pieces on a pad or your underwear.
  • Your vulva, the outside of your genitals, may look different or more open.

What can help?

  • During the first 24 hours, apply a cold pack wrapped in a towel for 15 to 20 minutes at a time, up to once an hour.
  • Rinse with warm water from a squeeze bottle when using the bathroom, and wipe from front to back.
  • A shallow warm bath or sitz bath may help, including after a bowel movement. Follow any restrictions in your discharge instructions.
  • Change your pad whenever you use the bathroom, and at least every 4 hours.
  • Use a cushion for comfort when sitting.
  • Take pain medication as directed by your healthcare provider.
  • Ask your healthcare provider about pelvic floor exercises, including Kegels, during recovery.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • Healing does not seem to be improving.
  • New or severe pain, bleeding, or discharge.
  • Yellow, green, or foul-smelling discharge from the wound or vagina.
  • Fever, flu-like symptoms, or pain that does not improve with prescribed or recommended medication.

Afterpains and cramps

Changes you may notice

  • Period-like cramps, called afterpains, happen as the uterus returns toward its usual size over about 6 weeks.
  • The cramps often last a few days to a week.
  • They may be stronger while breastfeeding or if you have given birth before.

What can help?

  • Take pain medication as directed by your healthcare provider.
  • Try a warm compress on your abdomen.
  • Empty your bladder regularly.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • New, severe, or worsening abdominal pain.
  • New or severe bleeding or unusual discharge.
  • Pain that does not improve with medication.

Breast changes and feeding-related discomfort

Changes you may notice

  • As milk comes in during the first few days, breasts may feel swollen, warm, tender, or sore. Some people have little discomfort.
  • Engorgement means the breasts are overly full. They can feel hard, swollen, or throbbing, with tight or shiny skin and flatter nipples.
  • Engorgement may last 24 to 48 hours, but worsening symptoms need attention.

What can help?

  • Wear a supportive bra that is not tight, and use nursing pads if milk leaks.
  • Apply a cold compress for 10 to 15 minutes every couple of hours for comfort.
  • Take pain medication as directed by your healthcare provider.
  • If breastfeeding, feed frequently, generally at least 8 times in 24 hours, following your feeding plan.
  • If engorged, express a small amount of milk to soften the breast and ease pressure. Expressing a little before a feed can help your baby latch.
  • If breastfeeding, a warm shower or warm, moist cloth before a feed may help milk flow.
  • If formula feeding, tenderness usually improves over a few days. Avoid warm compresses on the breasts.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • A sore, hard, red, or painful area on a breast.
  • Breasts that stay red, hot, swollen, and painful for more than 48 hours.
  • Painful or cracked nipples, or concerns about your baby’s latch.
  • Fever or feeling unwell. Do not wait 48 hours if these occur.

Constipation and bowel movements

Changes you may notice

  • Constipation is common after birth, and passing stool may be uncomfortable.
  • The first bowel movement may take 2 to 3 days after a vaginal birth or 3 to 5 days after a caesarean birth.

What can help?

  • Drink water regularly. Pale yellow or clear urine can be a useful sign of hydration.
  • Go to the bathroom when you feel the urge.
  • Gradually add fibre from fruit, vegetables, and whole grains.
  • Gradually increase activity as your recovery allows.
  • Ask your healthcare provider about a stool softener or another medicine if needed. Use it as directed.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • Constipation continues beyond the expected timeframe.
  • Recommended medication is not helping.

Hemorrhoids

Changes you may notice

  • Hemorrhoids are swollen veins around the anus that can develop during pregnancy or pushing.
  • They can cause itching, pain, and bleeding, and often improve over a few weeks.

What can help?

  • Move regularly and avoid staying in one position for too long.
  • Drink water, gradually increase fibre, and avoid straining during bowel movements.
  • Clean gently from front to back. Hemorrhoid pads or suitable gentle wipes may be more comfortable than dry toilet paper.
  • Apply a wrapped cold pack for 10 to 20 minutes a few times a day.
  • Use hemorrhoid treatments and pain medication as directed by your healthcare provider.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • Pain continues or is not improving.
  • Hemorrhoids have not improved after a few weeks.

Urination and bladder changes

Changes you may notice

  • Early soreness can make urination uncomfortable, and temporary urine leakage may occur during recovery.
  • Changes in how often you urinate may occur. Persistent burning, difficulty emptying your bladder, or inability to urinate should not be dismissed as normal.

What can help?

  • Use warm water from a squeeze bottle to rinse the perineum while using the bathroom.
  • Drink water regularly.
  • Running water in the sink may help you relax enough to urinate.
  • Ask about pelvic floor exercises, including Kegels, and take pain medication as directed.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • Ongoing pain or burning when urinating, or bladder pain in the lower abdomen.
  • Only small amounts of urine come out, or you cannot urinate. Seek prompt assessment rather than continuing home measures.
  • Urgency or frequent urination with burning.
  • Fever.
  • Urine leakage continues beyond 6 to 8 weeks.

Periods and fertility

Changes you may notice

  • If exclusively breastfeeding, your period may not return while your baby receives only breast milk. It often returns around the time solids start, but this varies.
  • If formula feeding, periods often return within 6 to 12 weeks.
  • Ovulation can happen before your first period returns, so pregnancy is possible before you have a period.

What can help?

  • Discuss contraception and the timing of another pregnancy with your healthcare provider.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • You have questions about your period, contraception, or changes in bleeding.

Recovery after a caesarean birth

Changes you may notice

  • Recovery can take about 6 weeks or longer, depending on your circumstances.
  • Fatigue, incision and lower abdominal pain, discomfort with movement, and vaginal bleeding can occur.
  • Follow your wound-care instructions. Dissolving stitches may disappear on their own; staples need removal by a healthcare provider.

What can help?

  • Take pain medication as directed and rest when possible.
  • Ask for help with lifting, bending, housework, and other tasks that strain your incision.
  • Follow your discharge instructions for showering and gently pat the incision dry. Wait to bathe until the incision has healed and your healthcare team says it is safe.
  • Do not lift anything heavier than your baby for the first 6 weeks, unless your healthcare team gives you different instructions.
  • Support your abdomen with a pillow when coughing or sneezing. A pillow can also help support your baby during feeding.
  • Move gently and regularly, increasing activity gradually.
  • Avoid high-impact exercise and activities that strain your abdomen during early recovery.
  • Ask your healthcare provider when it is safe to drive.

When to contact your healthcare provider

  • Pain does not improve with medication, or you feel increasingly unwell.
  • Fever.
  • The incision becomes red, warm, swollen, more painful, or starts draining.
  • Vaginal bleeding increases.
  • Pain, redness, or swelling in your calf, behind your knee, thigh, or groin. Seek urgent assessment.

When help cannot wait

Call 911 for chest pain, difficulty breathing, a seizure, fainting or passing out, or an immediate risk of harm to yourself or your baby.

Very heavy bleeding with dizziness or feeling faint needs emergency help. Do not drive yourself. Soaking a pad in less than an hour or passing large clots also needs immediate emergency assessment.

A severe or unusual headache with new vision changes needs immediate medical assessment. Do not assume it is only tiredness. Call 911 if you cannot travel safely or feel unsafe.

These are examples, not a complete list. You do not need to know the diagnosis before asking for help.
